# Backlog

Every open item across this repository's documents, condensed to one line each with a pointer to
where it is argued in full. Nothing here is new analysis — it is an index of decisions and work
that are already written down and not yet done, so a session can start from a list rather than a
re-read.

Status vocabulary: **defect** — the code is wrong; **decision** — nothing is wrong until someone
chooses; **work** — agreed, designed, unbuilt; **gap** — untested or unmeasured.

**An item's number is its name and never moves.** Other pages cite rows by id, so a row keeps its
number for life and a retired number is never reissued. Ordering carries the priority instead:
[What to do next](#what-to-do-next) ranks across the whole page, and within each section the rows
are ordered by what a player would notice first, which is why the numbers run out of sequence.

Every row here is open. What was fixed and why is recorded in the change log of the page that owns
it, which is also the page the **Where** column names — a finished item kept on two pages drifts on
one of them, and this one had drifted.

| B32 | **Reopened and re-answered 2026-08-25: the extinguisher cools blocks, and the measurement that closed this row was answering the wrong question.** It was closed as *hand-scale action is out of scope* on the ground that no hand-held tool can cool a ship — 26 five-kilogram CO2 bottles to return the median cooking block to ambient, 20 discharging at once to beat its 32 s window. **That is true and it is not what the tool is for.** The intent is **damage mitigation**: expendable ammunition that pulls one block back from the brink at a cost, not a way to run a ship hot. Re-measured against that question, the numbers invert — the solver damages a block at `(T − critical) × OverheatDamagePerKelvin`, so what stops the damage is the *overshoot*, tens of kelvin where the rise from ambient is hundreds: **0.5 bottles pulls the median block 10 K back from its rating and 2.4 pulls it 50 K**. **And it stays an emergency tool rather than a cooling system**, because the same table says 15.9 bottles at p90 and 90 at worst for that same 10 K — half a bottle saves an ordinary block and ninety do nothing for a capital reactor, so building for heat is still the answer to heat. | B43 | **Undecided: what refilling a coolant loop costs. Three routes, none chosen.** The direction is settled — venting coolant is a real way to shed heat and refilling it must cost something, which prices the grind-and-reweld exploit without touching the thermal model and leaves genuine mass ejection intact. What is undecided is the currency. **(1) A conveyor port on the pump.** Standard idiom, automatable, and a base with an ice farm refills itself — which reinforces *a base cools better*. Against it: it forces plumbing to reach a block whose position is already constrained by the ring, and the pump is an `UpgradeModuleDefinition`, which does not carry a conveyor port without changing what kind of block it is. **A variant avoids the plumbing**: the pump already attaches its power sink in code *because an upgrade module has no definition field for one*, and an inventory attaches the same way — so a player hand-loads it and a conveyor becomes optional rather than required. **(2) A coolant component in the build list, eaten to keep the loop full.** The mechanism that makes this work is that **damage destroys components where grinding returns them** — so venting *damages* the pipes, the coolant is genuinely lost, and the player welds it back from fresh stock. It handles combat for free and needs no inventory. Against it: damage changes a block's component mass, and mass is thermal capacity, so an eaten pipe is thermally lighter than a full one; and it is the per-block durability churn this idea was already suspected of. **(3) Say it is magic: energy and time, and nothing else.** By far the smallest — no component, no inventory, no conveyor, no damage coupling, nothing to haul. **And it may be the most honest of the three**, because the energy does not vanish: a pump's waste fraction is **1** by design — *a circulator does no work that leaves the system* — so power spent refilling becomes heat **in the ship**. Dumping heat then pays in the currency it is trying to cheat, and the exploit becomes a heat pump with a coefficient of performance, which is what it physically is. Set the cost so the refill's waste heat is a real fraction of the heat vented and the compounding gain shrinks; set it at one and the exploit is net negative. Time bounds the rate. Against it: it is a number chosen rather than derived, and it ties nothing to the ice economy. | [document-of-intent.md](document-of-intent.md#a-cooling-system-costs-power-and-makes-heat-doing-it), [thermal-model.md](thermal-model.md#coolant-loops) |
| B44 | **What is true of `B43` whichever route is chosen.** **An empty loop must remain a loop** — holding no coolant and transporting nothing, but still existing so it can be refilled — because *the loop stops existing* is the exact shape this area has failed in twice, once at 190 MJ through a ground pump. **Cost proportional to ring length is what turns the exploit into a feature**: the heat a vented ring dumps is `C_loop × ΔT` and `C_loop` scales with its pipes, so a per-pipe price makes the exchange rate constant and no loop size cheap to abuse. **Partial fill has a stiffness cliff**: a loop's substep demand is `SegmentConductance / EffectiveLoopMass`, so a 5 %-full loop is twenty times stiffer on an element that already competes to be a grid's worst, against a cap of 64 — either scale the contact conductance with fill too, which is more physical and makes the ratio invariant, or make fill **binary**, primed or dry, where a dry loop is thermally inert and never presents a small capacity at all. **Draining on damage needs no bookkeeping**: a holed ring already dissolves and the solver detects it today. **`A12` was fixed the other way and this row now says what that costs.** The spill kept 32.5 % of a dissolved ring's heat — neither venting it nor keeping it — and now keeps all of it: the pipe holds the parcel it absorbed, capacity and all, as `ThermalNode.HeldCoolantCapacity`. **Two variants were considered and are recorded because their failure modes are instructive.** *Coolant as a component of every pipe, first in the build order so damage takes it first*: the order is backwards — a block loses components in reverse, so the **last** entry goes first, which is why the pump's critical component sits fifth of six — and more fundamentally the mod cannot remove a component from a built block, and per-pipe tracking is the grid-durability churn the idea was already suspected of. *Coolant as a component of the pump alone*: a component list is fixed per **definition** and a loop's need scales with its **ring**, so a recipe can only ever charge one amount for a four-pipe ring and a fifty-pipe one alike.

**Cost proportional to ring length is the one that turns the exploit into the feature.** The heat a vented ring dumps is `C_loop × ΔT` and `C_loop` is proportional to its pipes, so charging coolant per pipe prices the yield exactly: dumping twice the heat costs twice the ice, whatever size loop you do it with. **That is mass ejection as a priced mechanic rather than an exploit to be blocked** — which is what the intent asked for.

**Damage draining a loop needs no per-block bookkeeping either.** Blowing a hole in a ring already dissolves the loop, which the solver detects today; *the loop dries and its coolant goes with the hole* falls out of that with no component stack touched and no durability updates at all.

**Binary priming is the smaller first step and it sidesteps the stiffness problem entirely.** A ring is either primed — full coolant, exactly today's behaviour — or dry, in which case it is thermally inert: holds nothing, exchanges nothing, and never presents a small capacity to the stability estimate at all. It still *exists* as a structure so it can be primed, which is the *an empty loop must remain a loop* rule above. That prices the exploit completely, needs no fill fraction and no efficiency curve, and leaves partial fill as a later refinement rather than a prerequisite. **If partial fill does come**, the efficiency curve then needs no inventing: capacity falls with fill so it buffers less, contact falls with fill so it absorbs slower, and flow falls with fill so it carries less — three consequences of one number rather than an authored coefficient. **And it reinforces a decision already taken**: a base can sit on an ice patch and a deep-space ship must carry its coolant, which is *a base cools better than a ship* arriving from the economy rather than from the physics. | [document-of-intent.md](document-of-intent.md#a-cooling-system-costs-power-and-makes-heat-doing-it), [thermal-model.md](thermal-model.md#coolant-loops) |

## K. Feature milestones

**Four features the mod is most of the way to already**, and the reason is the same in each case:
they are functions of the per-face surface data the thermal model computes on every step. This
section is for capability the mod does not have, so nothing in it is ranked in
[What to do next](#what-to-do-next) — that list is for the mod's own stated goals failing to reach a
player, and none of these is a goal yet.

**They are one arc rather than separate items.** `K1` is the energy — apply the force the friction
term already accounts for. `K6` is the shape — make that force directionally real rather than a
projected area. `K10` is the game — a ship's top speed becomes an outcome of the two plus its
thrust, instead of a curve somebody authored. `K20` is the server — a client that simulates cheaply
while the server simulates properly, so twenty people can watch twenty grids. Each is worth building
without the ones after it; none of them is worth building twice.

**`K19` is under all of them.** The scope widened on 2026-08-25 to *a grid's surfaces meet an
environment*, with heat one consequence of that and switchable off entirely — which it is not today,
because the force this section is about is computed inside the thermal solver's own node loop.

**A milestone is a row and its tasks are the rows under it**, named in it. A milestone is done when
its tasks are.

**All three cross a line this repository has drawn**, and it is named here rather than discovered later:
[document-of-intent.md](document-of-intent.md#what-this-mod-deliberately-is-not) says the mod is
*not authoritative over the game's own systems*. Applying a force writes to the game's movement,
which is a system the mod has only ever read — `ThermalGridEnvironment` takes
`Grid.Physics.LinearVelocity` and nothing anywhere writes to `Physics`. It is not unprecedented
(the mod already damages blocks, colours them and plays sounds) and it is not a small change of
character either, and `K10` goes furthest by making a ship's top speed this mod's answer rather than
the game's. **Whether the mod should push a ship at all is `K1`'s first question and not an
assumption inside it.**
