The aim of this page is a defensible range for every dial.

What makes a hot spot

arrangement, with total heat held constant

A hull's totals cannot explain why heat strands in two blocks: two ships with the same installed power and the same exposed area behave completely differently depending on whether the generators line the skin or stack in one bay. The census measures the arrangement — burial in conduction hops, spread about the watt-weighted centroid, and the worst watts per exposed square metre in any 5×5×5 neighbourhood.

Every raw correlation here is mostly “this ship makes more heat”, so total waste is partialled out below. What survives that is arrangement.

Can a block survive itself?

—

One number per block, computed from the definition alone with no simulation: the heat it makes at full rating over the most it could possibly shed while staying at its own critical temperature. The denominator is the best case that exists — every face radiating to deep space and every face bolted to armour held at ambient, both at once — so a block above 1 has no hull, no arrangement and no cooling that saves it. Only the definition can change.

The second column is the same ratio against its own skin alone. Above 1 there, the block cannot cool itself and depends on the hull taking the rest — which is a weaker position than it sounds, because heat only reaches hull area that is near it.

The standing panel

—

Thirty-six ships, each chosen by a named rule so the panel can be audited rather than trusted. It carries both grid sizes, both failure modes, the hulls that are critical in exactly one scenario — where a moved dial changes a verdict rather than a number — and controls that must stay safe whatever else is done. Findings

from these three passes

One block type decides the load criterion

LargeJumpDrive is 67 % of all the waste heat the corpus makes at full electrical load — 10,567 of them across 2,183 ships at 4.8 MW each — and its effect is binary. Of the 2,255 ships carrying one, 100.0 % reach 400 K and 100.0 % lose a block. Of the 5,887 without, 65.6 % and 21.5 %. At idle the two groups are indistinguishable — 0.2 % critical either way, medians of 179 and 177 K — because a drive is classed as a tool, and tools are off when parked.

The mechanism is the load state rather than the block. Full sets Tools = 1, so every drive on the hull charges at its full 32 MW draw for the whole 1,800 s clock and 15 % of that is heat; a real drive charges and then stops. G2 is not invalidated — it holds at 65.6 % on ships with no drive at all, well past its 20 % gate — but any tuning read off the aggregate is tuning for one block. Whether charging a drive should be a thermal event is a design question; that it should be unsurvivable for every hull that mounts one is probably not.

jump-drive ships 100.0 % critical (n=2,255) · others 21.5 % (n=5,887) · both 0.2 % at idle · median peak 1,574 K against 511 K

Large hulls fail by burial; small hulls fail by having nothing to shed with

Split by grid size, the same correlation table tells two different stories. On large grids buried share is a strong predictor (+0.571) and size still carries +0.488 — capital hulls wrap their machinery in themselves. On small grids burial barely registers (+0.145) and size almost drops out (+0.285); what decides is radiating surface per kilowatt and heat capacity per watt, both at −0.84.

That is two different fixes. Conduction and exposed-surface dials act on the buried case; heat capacity and waste fraction act on the starved one. A single global multiplier tuned on the aggregate will overshoot one population to reach the other.

The worst block in the game is a definition accident

LargePrototechReactor carries the TypeId HydrogenEngine in the game's own definition, so the derivation charges a 400 MW plant a combustion engine's 0.60 waste fraction. That is 240 MW of heat out of a 3×2×2 block: an index of 17.9, twelve times worse than anything else, and a self index of 46. At the reactor family's own 0.01 it would make 4 MW and be unremarkable.

This is the same shape as the reactor-waste bug already on record: the derivation table keys on the block's type, and the type is decided by the game's component wiring rather than by what the block is called. A definition can be internally consistent and still be attached to the wrong family. It needs a per-subtype override, which is the cheapest fix on this page.

found by the index, not by the corpus — it is checkable from the definition in milliseconds

A jump drive is survivable in principle and never in practice

Its index is 0.75: bolted to armour that stays at ambient it survives, because superconductors conduct extremely well. Its self index is 7.4 — its own skin sheds an eighth of what it makes — so it lives or dies on the hull absorbing the rest.

The hull never does. Of 2,179 corpus ships carrying one, 909 have more than the 19,068 m² of 400 K armour the index says is needed to absorb its export, and all 909 still lose a block. Total hull area is irrelevant when the heat cannot travel: block-to-block conductance is about 112 W/K, so moving megawatts even one block needs thousands of kelvin. Area only counts if it is near the source — which is the local-density finding arriving from the other direction.

The clumping index does not work — local density does

The obvious spatial statistic for “how clustered are the generators” is the Clark-Evans nearest-neighbour index, and measured against the hot spot with total heat partialled out it lands at −0.033. It is useless. Evenness of spacing is not the mechanism: two 5 MW generators ten metres apart score as dispersed and still cook the bay they share.

What works is local watts against local radiating area — +0.369 against the hot spot and +0.552 against the peak, an order of magnitude more predictive. That is the quantity a block actually experiences, and it bands into a usable rule: under 500 W/m² locally, 0.7 % of ships lose a block; between 10,000 and 50,000, 93.2 %.

Spreading the sources out helps, but only in the middle of the range. At matched total heat, the most-spread third loses a block 21.7 % of the time against 45.2 % for the tightest at 100 kW–1 MW, and 61.6 % against 91.5 % at 1–10 MW. Past about 10 MW installed it is 98–100 % either way: arrangement buys a builder a great deal until it buys nothing.

Heat capacity and emissivity are orthogonal, which makes them tunable separately

Measured on the panel: scaling heat capacity moves when and not where. Time to the first lost block tracks it almost exactly — one ship goes 2, 4, 7, 14, 28 seconds across a sixteenfold range — while its peak stays pinned. Scaling emissivity does the reverse: the peak falls and the blocks lost drop sharply, while the time hardly moves.

So the survey's two complaints have separate dials. "Damage arrives in six seconds" is a heat capacity question. "Ships reach absurd temperatures" is an emissivity and exposure question. The one place this breaks down is a buried block, where emissivity does nothing at all — a hull with no exposed face has nothing to radiate from, whatever its surface is set to.

What this can and cannot tell you

The dials are swept one at a time, so what you get is a set of partial derivatives and not a model of the whole space. Two dials moved together will not in general do what their curves added together say: emissivity and exposed area multiply inside the radiation term, and heat capacity trades against waste fraction inside the time constant. The curves are for finding the range a dial should live in, and for ranking dials against one another. Treat anything past that as a ballpark and confirm it with a run.

Peaks above critical are censored. The harness never destroys an overheating block, so past critical it keeps generating and keeps climbing for the rest of the clock. Read those as "this block dies" and nothing more. Times to critical are decided at the crossing and are unaffected, which is why the findings rest on them.

Timings are seconds of play. HeatTimeScale is 225, so nine seconds at the controls is about thirty-four minutes of real heating.

G3 is still unanswered. No ship in the corpus carries a radiator or a coolant loop, because the corpus filter rejects any ship with a non-vanilla block. It needs a retrofit pass that fits a standard cooling kit and re-runs the panel.
