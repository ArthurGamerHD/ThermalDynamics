Findings

from this run

Three things the data says that the passing criteria do not.

Peak temperature above critical is a lab artifact, not a prediction

The solver raises an overheat event when a block passes its critical temperature, but applying that damage lives only in the game layer — DoDamage is called from ThermalGridSimulation, which the harness never runs. In the lab an overheating block is never destroyed, so it keeps generating heat and keeps climbing for the rest of the clock. In game it would be gone within seconds and would stop producing.

That invalidates the entire right tail. A ship reported at 541,648 K did not get hot; it ran an undamped source for 1,800 s that the real game would have removed almost immediately. Every peak above critical should be read as “this block dies” and nothing more, and the population statistics that quote peak — p95, p99, max — describe the lab, not the mod.

21.6 % of rows exceed 900 K · 3.9 % exceed 2,000 K · 1,076 of 8,054 ships have at least one runaway scenario · 83.5 % of runaway rows never settle within the clock

Damage arrives in seconds, so there is no window to react

Under full electrical load the median ship that overheats at all loses its first block after 9 s; burning forward, after 6 s. The tenth percentile is three to four seconds. Heat is meant to be a resource a player reasons about, but a consequence that lands this fast is not a resource — it is a trap, indistinguishable from the block simply exploding when switched on.

The distribution is also bimodal in a telling way: at idle the same measure is 112 s. Nothing in between. Ships are either thermally inert or they fail instantly, which is exactly the shape that leaves no room for play.

These are seconds of play, not of physical time — HeatTimeScale is 225, so nine seconds at the controls is about thirty-four minutes of real heating. The compression is deliberate; this run is the first measurement of where it left the hottest designs. Turning the dial down is not the fix, because it slows the inert ships equally and the gap between the two groups is only about twelvefold. What separates them is per-block: a producer whose waste watts are large against its own heat capacity crosses critical almost at once, whatever the clock says.

time to first critical block, ships that reach it · full-electrical median 9 s (p10 4 s) · burn-forward median 6 s (p10 3 s) · idle median 112 s

The heat concentrates in one or two blocks, and it is usually the same block

Runaway rows carry a median of 2 blocks above critical while the rest of the hull sits near its median temperature — on humpback 38 the peak is 164,570 K against a median of 360 K. The heat is not spreading; it is stranded in the block that made it, with no conduction path fast enough to move it and no exposed face to radiate it.

LargeHydrogenEngine is the hottest block on 34 % of runaway rows while being the hottest on only 4 % of rows overall. Alongside LargeJumpDrive and the large thrusters, a short list of block types accounts for most of the tail. That makes this tunable without touching the solver: waste-heat fraction, thermal mass, or conductance to neighbours, on perhaps a dozen definitions.

median hotspot blocks on runaway rows: 2 · on ordinary rows: 610 · 58 parked ships generate over 1 MW at idle, one at 24 MW

How to read this

Blocks lost and first loss are measured at the shipped per-block critical temperatures and do not move with the slider. Peak, and every criterion computed from it, are recomputed against the threshold you set — that is a proxy for re-tuning critical temperature uniformly, not a re-simulation, and it cannot tell you what a changed threshold would do to the heat flow itself. Only a re-run can do that.

The corpus is walked largest-first, so any partial result is drawn from capital hulls and reads wildly high. These figures are from a complete run. G3 (cooling works) needs a retrofit pass, because the corpus filter rejects any ship carrying a non-vanilla block and therefore no ship here has a radiator. G4 (design decides, not size) needs exposed area per watt, which this dataset does not carry; block count alone correlates with heat at Spearman +0.012, so the variance lives somewhere a builder controls.
